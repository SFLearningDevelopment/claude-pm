Step 1 of 5
The problem
Example 11 · Tier 3Contract evaluationVendor · LegalSigning deadline

The winning vendor from the Meridian AI Assistant RFP has sent their Master Services Agreement — 40 pages, standard legal language, sent on a Friday with a requested signing date of the following Wednesday. Legal at SF is backed up with two other contracts. Sarah has until Tuesday to either sign, request changes, or escalate.

Sarah is not a lawyer. Her job is not to interpret the contract — that is legal's role. Her job is to identify the clauses that need immediate legal attention so the limited legal review time is focused on the highest-risk items, and to brief Raj Patel on what she found before he talks to the vendor.

Contract context — what Sarah knows
Vendor: CloudMind AI (winner of RFP, selected for RAG pipeline component of Meridian AI Assistant)
Contract value: $180K over 18 months
Meridian context: Financial services firm, SOC 2 Type II required, PII cannot be stored on third-party infrastructure, data residency requirement (US only)
SF context: SF is the prime contractor — CloudMind is a subcontractor. SF carries liability to Meridian.
Signing deadline: Wednesday (4 days away)
Key concern: SF's liability exposure as prime contractor if CloudMind underperforms or causes a data incident
Why this is Tier 3: The PM must reason across multiple inputs simultaneously — contract language, the client's compliance requirements, SF's liability position as prime contractor, and a hard signing deadline — and produce a prioritised brief that is specific enough to direct legal review without overstepping into legal advice. Claude cannot replace a lawyer but can dramatically accelerate the triage.

Step 2 of 5
The naive approach

The naive approach is to skim the contract, note a few things that look unusual, and forward it to legal with a covering email:

Sarah (naive — email to legal)
Hi — can you review the attached MSA from CloudMind AI? We need to sign by Wednesday. Key things I noticed: their liability cap seems low and they have some IP clauses worth checking. Let me know if you see anything. Thanks.
What's wrong: "Liability cap seems low" and "some IP clauses worth checking" give legal no direction. A 40-page MSA will take 4–6 hours to review properly. Without a prioritised brief, legal will read sequentially — and may spend significant time on boilerplate while missing the clauses that actually matter for this specific engagement. The PII and data residency requirements — which are specific to Meridian and critical given SF's prime contractor liability — are not mentioned at all.

Step 3 of 5
The Claude interaction

Sarah pastes the key contract clauses into Claude along with the project context and asks for a structured red flag analysis — not legal advice, but a prioritised list of clauses that need attention given the specific engagement context.

Sarah — Turn 1
I'm a senior PM (not a lawyer) reviewing a vendor MSA before passing it to legal for review. I need to identify the highest-risk clauses given my specific engagement context so legal review time is focused correctly. I am NOT asking for legal advice. I need a structured list of clauses that warrant legal attention, with the specific reason each clause is relevant to my context. Engagement context: — Vendor is a subcontractor to SF; SF is prime contractor to Meridian (financial services) — SF carries liability to Meridian if the vendor causes a data incident — Meridian requires: SOC 2 Type II, PII not stored on third-party infrastructure, US data residency only — Contract value: $180K / 18 months — Signing deadline: 4 days Key contract clauses (excerpts): Section 8.2 — Liability: "CloudMind's total liability under this agreement shall not exceed the fees paid in the 3 months preceding the claim." Section 12.1 — Data processing: "CloudMind may process customer data on infrastructure located within the United States or the European Union as operationally required." Section 14.3 — Subcontracting: "CloudMind may engage subcontractors to perform services under this agreement without prior written consent of the customer." Section 9.1 — Indemnification: "Each party shall indemnify the other against third-party claims arising from its own gross negligence or wilful misconduct." Section 11.2 — Security: "CloudMind will maintain security controls consistent with industry standards." Section 15.4 — SLA: "CloudMind will use commercially reasonable efforts to maintain 99% uptime. Credits for downtime shall be the customer's sole remedy for availability failures." Section 6.3 — IP: "Any deliverables created by CloudMind under this agreement shall remain the intellectual property of CloudMind, licensed to the customer on a non-exclusive basis."
Claude — Turn 1
Contract Red Flag Analysis — CloudMind AI MSA For legal review prioritisation — not legal advice 🔴 CRITICAL — Requires immediate legal attention before signing Section 12.1 — Data processing (EU data residency) The clause permits data processing in the EU "as operationally required." Meridian's requirement is US data residency only. This clause directly conflicts with the client requirement. If a data incident occurs on EU infrastructure, SF's liability to Meridian is not mitigated by this contract. Legal should either require deletion of "or the European Union" or add a rider restricting processing to US infrastructure only. Section 8.2 — Liability cap (3-month fees) The cap is fees paid in the preceding 3 months — approximately $10K on an $180K/18-month contract. For an AI component processing financial services data, a breach or data loss event could expose SF to client liability far exceeding $10K. Legal should negotiate a higher cap (typically 12 months' fees is standard) or at minimum carve out data breach events from the cap. Section 14.3 — Unlimited subcontracting CloudMind can engage subcontractors without SF's consent. If a subcontractor processes Meridian's PII on non-compliant infrastructure, SF carries the liability. Legal should require prior written consent for any subcontractor handling Meridian data, and flow-down of the data residency and SOC 2 requirements to subcontractors. 🟡 AMBER — Review recommended Section 9.1 — Indemnification (gross negligence only) Indemnification is limited to gross negligence or wilful misconduct. Ordinary negligence — a misconfigured system, an unpatched vulnerability — would not trigger indemnification. For a financial services data processing context, legal should consider whether this standard is appropriate or whether a lower threshold should apply. Section 11.2 — Security controls ("industry standards") "Industry standards" is undefined and unenforceable as written. Given Meridian's SOC 2 Type II requirement, this should explicitly reference SOC 2 Type II compliance, with an obligation to provide audit reports on request. "Consistent with industry standards" gives SF no contractual basis to require SOC 2 evidence. Section 15.4 — SLA credits as sole remedy If CloudMind's availability failure causes SF to miss a client milestone or SLA, the sole remedy is credits — not damages. Legal should assess whether this is acceptable given SF's upstream SLA commitments to Meridian. Section 6.3 — IP ownership Deliverables remain CloudMind's IP, licensed non-exclusively. If the RAG pipeline is customised for Meridian's data and CloudMind is later acquired or goes out of business, SF has no ownership of the custom components. Legal should consider whether a work-for-hire clause or ownership transfer is required for Meridian-specific deliverables.
What changed: Legal now has a prioritised brief — three critical items and four amber items, each with the specific reason it matters for this engagement. A 4-hour review can be focused on the critical items first. Sarah has not given legal advice — she has given direction. The data residency conflict was entirely missed in the naive approach and is the single highest-risk clause in the contract.

Step 5 of 5
Watch out
1. Claude is not a lawyer — and this is not a legal opinion
The red flag analysis is a triage tool, not a legal opinion. Claude can identify clauses that are unusual or that conflict with stated requirements — but it cannot assess enforceability, jurisdiction-specific implications, or negotiating strategy. Everything Claude flags must be reviewed by qualified legal counsel before any signing decision.
2. Claude only sees what you paste
A 40-page MSA cannot be fully pasted into a Claude session (context window limits). Sarah pasted the key clauses she identified on a skim read. If she missed a clause, Claude cannot flag it. Always do a manual skim first to identify candidate clauses — use Claude to analyse what you've found, not to replace the initial read.
3. Standard clauses look standard until they aren't
Section 14.3 (unlimited subcontracting) is extremely common in SaaS contracts and easy to overlook. It only becomes a red flag when combined with PII processing requirements. Claude identified it because Sarah provided the engagement context. Without that context, Claude would have no basis to flag it.
The discipline that makes this work:
Provide the engagement context before the contract clauses — not after. Claude cannot identify what matters without knowing why it matters. The context (PII requirements, data residency, prime contractor liability) is what turns a generic contract review into a targeted risk analysis.
