Step 1 of 5
The problem
Example 1 · Tier 1 Project scoping Single-context 2–3 turn interaction

Sarah Chen is the SF Senior PM on the Meridian Financial Group account. Three workstreams are running concurrently — Cloud Migration, Meridian Portal, and the Meridian AI Assistant. It's Week 1 of the Portal engagement.

Mark Donovan, Meridian's CTO, has sent over the project brief. It's two paragraphs of executive notes typed directly into an email. No structured requirements. No exclusions. No success criteria.

Source document — Mark Donovan's brief (verbatim email)

Hi Sarah — as discussed, we want to rebuild the client portal. The current one is embarrassing. Clients should be able to see their account, run reports, upload documents, and ideally get some AI help with their queries. We want it to feel modern. Mobile too. The team needs to be able to manage users from their end. Timeline is aggressive — Mark wants it done by Q3. Budget TBD but let's not go crazy. Lisa will be your day-to-day. Let me know what you need from us.


One more thing — compliance is important for us (we're a financial services firm) and whatever we build needs to integrate with our existing Salesforce setup. Don't break anything.

Sarah has a kickoff call with Lisa Park (Meridian PMO Lead) in 48 hours. She needs a structured scope document to walk through — one that captures what's in, what's out, what's ambiguous, and what she needs answers on before the team can begin.

Why this is harder than it looks: A vague brief isn't just incomplete — it's a risk surface. "Don't break anything" could mean anything. "Q3" is six months away or four depending on what month it is. "Ideally get some AI help" could be a chatbot or a full RAG pipeline. The scope document Sarah produces will become the basis for the SOW, the sprint plan, and every future scope-creep conversation.

Step 2 of 5
The naive approach

Under time pressure, most PMs reach for a scope document template — or ask Claude with a single generic prompt.

Sarah (naive prompt)
Here's a project brief from my client. Can you turn it into a scope document? [pastes email]

What Claude produces from this prompt is technically a scope document. It has the right headings. But it has a critical flaw:

What goes wrong: Claude fills in the gaps with plausible-sounding assumptions. "Mobile too" becomes "fully responsive PWA with offline support." "AI help with queries" becomes "integrated AI chatbot using OpenAI." "Don't break anything" disappears entirely. The document looks complete — but it contains commitments Sarah never made and omits risks she should have flagged.

The naive output is dangerous precisely because it looks professional. Lisa Park will read it and assume it reflects an informed assessment. When the AI chatbot scope gets challenged in Week 6, Sarah will have no documented basis for the assumption.

The pattern to recognise: When Claude is given an ambiguous input with no structure, it defaults to making the output look complete by filling gaps with reasonable assumptions. For scoping work, this is the opposite of what a PM needs. You need the gaps surfaced, not hidden.

Step 3 of 5
The Claude interaction

Sarah approaches this differently. Instead of asking Claude to write the scope document, she asks it to help her analyse the brief first, then structure the output with explicit handling of ambiguity.

Sarah — Turn 1
I'm a senior PM at a cloud consultancy. I've received a project brief from a client (financial services sector) for a portal rebuild. Before we write anything, I need you to read this brief and tell me: 1. What is explicitly stated as in scope 2. What is implied but not confirmed 3. What is missing that I need to clarify before the kickoff call 4. Any red flags or risks in the brief itself Do not make assumptions. Flag everything uncertain as uncertain. [pastes email]
Claude — Turn 1
Here's my analysis of the brief: Explicitly stated (in scope): — Account visibility for clients — Report running capability — Document upload — User management for internal team — Mobile support — Salesforce integration — Compliance requirement (unspecified standard) Implied but unconfirmed: — "AI help with queries" — could be keyword search, a chatbot, or a RAG-based assistant. These have very different build costs and timelines. — "Modern feel" — UX/design scope is entirely undefined — "Q3 deadline" — Q3 of which year? Which month is the hard deadline? — "Budget TBD" — no budget ceiling means no basis for trade-off decisions Missing — needs clarification before kickoff: — Which compliance framework? (SOC 2, PCI DSS, FINRA?) — Salesforce integration depth? (read-only sync, bi-directional, real-time?) — What does "don't break anything" protect specifically? — Who are the end users? Clients only, or also internal staff? — What is the current portal built on and what is being replaced vs. extended? — Is there an existing design system or brand guide? Red flags: — "Timeline is aggressive" with "budget TBD" is a constraint conflict. Budget needs to be established before timeline can be confirmed. — "Ideally get some AI help" signals an aspirational feature that hasn't been scoped. This will become scope creep if not explicitly defined or excluded now. — Compliance is mentioned as important but with no specification — this is a delivery risk.

Sarah reviews Claude's analysis — it's close, but she wants to add one more constraint she knows from her discovery call: the portal must not store PII on SF infrastructure. She refines:

Sarah — Turn 2
Good. One thing to add to red flags: the client mentioned verbally that PII cannot be stored on third-party infrastructure — this needs to be in the scope document as a constraint. Now write the scope document. Use these sections: — Project overview (2–3 sentences) — In scope (bullet list, confirmed items only) — Out of scope (explicit exclusions) — Assumptions (things we're proceeding with pending confirmation) — Open questions (must be resolved at kickoff) — Constraints — Success criteria (draft — to be confirmed with client) Do not fill in anything that isn't in the brief or my additions. Mark anything uncertain with [TO CONFIRM].

Step 5 of 5
Watch out

Three failure modes to watch for when using Claude for scoping work:

1. Assumption laundering
Claude's default behaviour with vague input is to produce plausible-looking output by filling gaps silently. Always explicitly instruct Claude to flag uncertainty rather than resolve it. The phrase "do not make assumptions — mark everything uncertain as uncertain" should be in every scoping prompt.
2. The complete-looking document trap
A Claude-generated scope document will almost always look well-structured and professional. This is dangerous — it creates false confidence. Always review the output asking: "Where has Claude resolved something I haven't actually confirmed?" If you can't answer that, you haven't reviewed it carefully enough.
3. Verbal-only context
Information from verbal conversations (like Sarah's PII constraint) does not exist for Claude unless you tell it. Before any scoping session, gather your notes and add them explicitly. Claude has no memory of your discovery calls.
The prompting discipline that makes this work:
Analyse before writing. Ask Claude to surface gaps and flag ambiguity before it generates any output. Then provide confirmed context. Then generate. This two-phase approach produces a fundamentally better document than asking for the output directly.
